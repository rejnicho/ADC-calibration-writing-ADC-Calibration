\section{Abstract}
\label{sec:Abstract}

This work outlines a calibration of analog to digital converters using a differential nonlinearity informed method. 
Specifically, this method was developed for use in Rubin Observatory's LSST Camera but could be applied to other Analog to Digital Converters (ADCs). 
This method showcases the individual periodic structure of each ADC by examining its long range behavior. 
We aptly name this method the cumulative binning method, which aims to determine the bin edges of the ADCs. 
The method recovers important quantities of the ADCs, specifically differential and integral nonlinearity which fall within specifications from the manufacturer, Analog Devices\cite{ADCSpec}. 
The method demonstrates a robust calibration utilizing two types of testing data from the electro-optical testing runs for the LSST Camera, suggesting the calibration can be conducted on various testing data.
The results demonstrate the braided substructure of the ADCs, with good consistency between the calibration data run types. 

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec: Motivations}
\indent 


Rubin Observatory is a first of its kind observatory, uniquely designed as the descendant of many great predecessors such as GAIA \cite{GAIADR3, GAIAMission} known for its precision, and SLOAN \cite{Sloan1, Sloan2} known for the volume of its survey to ¼ the sky. 
Located in Cerro Pachón, Chile, Rubin Observatory’s Simonyi Survey Telescope is a modified Paul-Baker design with a 8.4m primary mirror. 
The design allows a 9.6 square degree field of view, which will observe in 6 broadband optical filters, ugrizy. 
Rubin Observatory will begin the Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) in 2025 to capture over 20,000 square degrees, with nearly 1000 visits to each location in the Southern sky over the 10 year survey. 
The survey strategy includes 30s visit times to each pointing on sky to achieve such coverage. 
The project aims to probe 4 central science themes: Probing Dark Energy and Dark Matter, Taking an Inventory of the Solar System, Exploring the Transient Optical Sky, and Mapping the Milky Way\cite{LSSToverview}.

The LSST Camera is the instrument to be installed in the upcoming Rubin Observatory.
The camera features 3200 megapixels over 189 scientific, fully depleted charge coupled devices (CCDs) and 12 corner CCDs acting as wavefront guides \cite{wavefront, Gilmore2008}. 
CCDs are a well studied device\cite{Janesick}, used in precursor camera systems such as Hubble's Wide Field Camera\cite{HSTCamera}, Dark Energy Survey's DECam \cite{DECam}, and Subaru's Hyper-Suprime Camera \cite{HyperSuprimeCam}.
Structurally, LSST Camera's CCDs are 100\textmu m to balance infrared response while minimizing diffusion in the sensors \cite{SiliconThickness}.
The sensors themselves have two manufacturers, E2V and ITL, which have been characterized through extensive testing to characterize their properties such as quantum efficiency, bright and dark defects, and charge transfer inefficiencies (CTE). \cite{CCDCharacterization1, CCDCharacterization2} 

The CCDs are arranged into rafts as 3x3 arrays, with groups of 3 CCDs connected to a readout electronic board (REB) \cite{RTA1, RTA2}.
Each pixel has a physical size of 10\textmu m x 10\textmu m, which corresponds to a 0.04 arcsec sq/pixel.\cite{CCDCharacterization2} 
Rubin Observatory’s survey plan aims for a full readout of the focal plane in under 2s to minimize dead time \cite{IntegratingLSSTCam}, requiring segmentation of each CCD into 16 amplifiers of over 1 million pixels with separate timing electronics. 

The camera was extensively tested through many iterations of electro-optical (EO) testing completed at SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory. 
The sensors themselves are characterized to understand their bias, saturation, linearity, and other properties.\cite{CameraCharacterization2024}. 
The voltages and timing electronics are also optimized for best operations and clarity of images to be taken on sky. \cite{FPOptimization2024}
Additional studies have investigated camera anomalies and interesting aspects such as weather in flat images \cite{BanovetzWeather}, photometry and psf measurements \cite{EstevesPhotometry}, the brighter fatter effect\cite{BroughtonBrighterFatter}, and crosstalk \cite{crosstalk}. 

The understanding of the entire camera system is integrated into an instrument signature removal (ISR) pipeline to recover accurate signal incident to the sensors \cite{ISR1}. 
From Plazas Malagon et al. \citep
{ISR1}, we clearly see that a gap exists in the ISR pipeline for the Differential Nonlinearity (DNL) correction, but a look-up table could sufficiently fill this missing area. 
Previous work such as for the Hubble Wide Field Camera demonstrates consideration for the ADC correction but does not clearly outline such a method for its computation. \cite{HSTCamera}
This work aims to fill that gap by creating an ADC calibration utilizing a DNL informed method. 
We will develop a calibration for each ADC used by Rubin Observatory's LSST Camera, which are Analog Devices 18-bit, 1 MSPS PulSAR ADCs \cite{ADCSpec}.
In this calibration, we will emphasize the importance of the differential nonlinearity (DNL) and the integral nonlinearity (INL), in order to properly represent the ADC behavior and match that to the specifications set by the manufacturer. 

In the following sections we will develop our method as outlined here. 
Initially, we will define DNL and INL, specify the criteria for calibrating data, and describe the data preparation process. 
We will explain the cumulative binning method via the probability distribution function in Section \ref{sec: Cummulative binning}.
We will then examine the result of our calibration in Section \ref{sec:results}, noting interesting differences between calibration data. 
Section \ref{sec:Future} will describe an application of such calibration, including a sample look-up table for the ADC correction. 
Finally, the appendix will note additional considerations as well as an earlier trial calibration of the ADCs.

\subsection{Definition of Differential and Integral Nonlinearity}
\label{sec: DNL_INL_Def}
\indent 


The analog to digital converter, as the name implies, converts the analog signal from a system into a digital one. 
The output signal is measured in analog to digital units (ADU). 
The input signal has limits, called edges, which map the input voltage range to a stated ADU value. 
These edges have a lower bound, called the left edge, and an upper bound, called the right edge.
The difference between the right edge and the left edge determines the width of the bin, and is determined through the process called calibration of the ADC. 
This calibration is dependent on the intrinsic illumination of the sensor which the ADC is attached, which specifies the conversion between this illumination and the ADU.

As seen in Figure \ref{fig:idealADC}, in a perfect ADC (red), the bins have a width of exactly 1 ADU, and are spaced integers apart. 
That is, an input signal 0-0.9 would be 0 ADU, 1.0-1.9 is 1 ADU, and so on. 
However, in a true ADC (black), bins may differ from the ideal bin width of 1 ADU and therefore may have non-integer left and right edges mapping to specific ADU values. 
As shown in Figure \ref{fig:idealADC}, voltage 0-1.2 would be 0 ADU, 1.3-1.8 is 1 ADU, and so on. 

The presence of deviations from an ideal ADC in terms of bin widths leads to two types of nonlinearity: differential (DNL) and integral (INL). 
DNL is the deviation of a bin's width from that of an ideal bin width and is defined exactly as
\begin{equation}
DNL =1- w  
\label{eq: DNL}
\end{equation}
where w is the width of a bin. 
This value is computed for all bins along the ADC. 
DNL values are typically on the order of $\pm$ 0.5, and any value greater than $\pm$ 1 could indicate a skipped bin. 
The manufacturer of the ADCs used on the LSST Camera, AD7982 \cite{ADCSpec}, state they have no missing bins/codes from the manufacturing, which will be verified in this analysis. 

The second type of nonlinearity is integral nonlinearity. 
This is the deviation of the middle of a bin from that of an ideal bin, which is denoted as
\begin{equation}
INL = m_{ideal} - m_{adc} 
\label{eq: INL}
\end{equation}
where $m_{ideal}$ is the midpoint of an ideal bin, and $m_{adc}$ is the midpoint of an ADC bin. 
INL typically yields contributions from each bin on the order of $\pm$ 1, with deviations above 2 being out of specification (AD7982, 2020). 
As in Figure \ref{fig:idealADC}, the first bin has DNL = 1.2-1 = 0.2, and INL = 0.5-0.6 = -0.1. 

These nonlinearities characterize the signal coming from the ADC and should be examined to see if any patterns emerge as structure along the ADC edges. 
For instance, a local portion of the ADC with DNL could also induce some local INL, simply by construction of their definitions.
While this may be expected, changes in one type of nonlinearity but not the other may signal an issue in the calibration or in the ADC itself.
We will examine any structure that is present through our calibration of LSST Camera's ADCs, especially nonlinearity outside of the expectation. 
In the coming sections, we will use information about the incident illumination to calibrate the ADC and determine both the DNL and INL for every ADC. 

\subsection{Run Selection and Filters}
\label{sec: RDF}
\indent 


Calibration of the ADCs for LSST Camera involves using signal from the CCD over its range, from bias to saturation, which is mapped to roughly 100k ADU. 
The CCDs collect charge as a function of incident light on the sensor, meaning any one image could only show some small subset of the range of the CCD and therefore only an equally small subset of ADU.
To cover the range of the ADC, a large number of exposures are needed to represent the range of the CCD. 
For our use case of the LSST Camera, we utilize collections of test exposures called runs as collected through EO testing completed at SLAC. 
Runs completed during the EO testing period are designed with specific calibrations and characterizations as their focus, which help inform ISR \cite{ISR1}. 
Therefore, in this calibration, we seek to use data suitable for understanding the ADC behavior.

The first data we deem usable is 13144, which is over 600 exposures of flat pairs with exposures covering bias to saturation of the CCDs. 
When compiled into a single histogram in counts per ADU, we can look at the distribution of codes represented by the ADC.  
Figure \ref{fig:13144Distributions} demonstrates one such combined distribution for our example amplifier, R22S00C01.
This plot shows fluctuations in the amount of signal present in each bin over the range of the ADC.
The bin-to-bin deviations are two-fold. 
The most present is the right skew of the signal, which corresponds to a high density of exposures with low exposure time.
This structure is the result of the log-based flux sampling used for the flat pairs and is coupled with the Poisson distribution spreading the distribution about its mean. 
This density structure is suitable for the intended use for this run and is a characteristic we will have to relax in order to use this run for our ADC calibration. 
There are also local bin-to-bin deviations that denote the presence of DNL in the ADC. 

The second type of run for this calibration is a ramp run, 13549. 
This special run was designed specifically to calibrate the ADCs. 
For all exposures in this run, after the set integration time has elapsed, the charge would begin to be read out while continuing integration of charge by leaving the illumination on. 
This is repeated three times for each of the 78 exposure times to cover the range of the ADC from bias through saturation of the CCDs.
Our sample distribution for our reference amplifier is shown in Figure \ref{fig:13549Hist}, demonstrating the the CCD's even coverage over its range.

After selecting two suitable runs to develop a calibration, we must consider how to model an ideal ADC for the given range. 
We want this model to represent the any long range behavior, such as our observed 128 bin low bins structure seen in Figure \ref{fig:13549Hist}, while minimizing bin-to bin deviations.  
This is accomplished by using a filter, in this case a Savitizy-Golay filter.
Our standard analysis is developed using a third order polynomial with a window size of 33, expressed beyond here as o3w33. 
We demonstrate the effect of this filter choice on both the ramp and flat pairs runs as seen in Figure \ref{fig:13549Hist} and the right plot of Figure \ref{fig:13144Distributions} respectively as the red curve superimposed over the histogram. 
Effectively, this demonstrates the preservation of long-range behavior and smoothing on smaller scales. 
Further, we use this filter as the model ADC to develop the method outlined in Section \ref{sec: Cummulative binning}. 

\subsection{Run Preparation}
\label{sec: Preparation}
\indent 


There are two requirements for the calibrating runs that must be considered before developing a calibration of the ADC.
The first requires covering the entire range of the CCD; that is, there must be signal for the bins to be calibrated. 
This criteria was satisfied by our selection of the flat pairs run 13144 and the ramp run 13549, as they both demonstrate signal from bias through saturation of the CCD.  
The second requirement is an even density of exposures over the range of the ADC; that is, the density of information must be consistent in order to prevent an arbitrarily skew the bin size. 

To more completely understand the density motivation we consider how signal is expected to fall into each of the ADC bins. 
We call the distribution of ADU values for a given amount of light the probability distribution Wfunction (pdf).
This is exactly how many pixels on the amplifier will yield a specific ADU value given some accumulated charge. 
We have no \textit{a priori} knowledge of this distribution and instead need to infer it from the signal we receive from the ADC.
As determined using the calibration method in Section \ref{sec: Cummulative binning}, the shape of the calibrating distribution over the range of the ADC contributes to the characterizing parameters such as the DNL and INL.
Therefore, utilizing a distribution where there is a large amount of exposures in a single region and few in another creates small bins (on average) with large DNL and an unstable (bin starting dependent) INL as seen in Section \ref{sec:Adrianmethod} in the Appendix. 

Given this need for a flat distribution and the base structure of our calibrating runs, we must prepare our runs for use. 
The first step in our preparations involves "trimming" the exposures to remove the signal from the pixels that fall on the outside edges of the CCDs. 
These pixels have diverging electric fields which cause the signal in this region to be less than its true signal. 
The need to conduct this trimming originates from a small peak in signal near the bias level in corner amplifiers, which could not be attributed to how the data was collected. 
To preserve as much signal as possible while removing this defect, we trim 2 pixels from each edge of the CCD in every exposure.
Testing demonstrated that 2 pixels was sufficient in removing this defect, while trimmings between 0-12 pixels were examined.
For consistency, and because a potential bias level peak would be masked in the skewed distribution of the flat pairs, we trim all exposures in both the ramp and flat pairs runs. 
This also allows for the preservation of as much signal as possible for the ADC calibration. 
We note that removal of the picture frame is also being studied as part of the ISR for LSST science image preparation, which differs from the requirement to conduct this calibration. 

The second aspect to address is the variable density of representation in 13144, or the left plot of Figure \ref{fig:13144Distributions}.
This requires determining the groupings of exposures that demonstrate the distribution of signal over the entire ADC. 
This problem was the motivating factor for the development of 13549, which was taken after this work to calibrate the ADCs began. 
While the ramp run avoids the density problem, it is not always practical to request a special run of exposures for this calibration alone given the time necessary for the run.
Therefore, we move to be able to utilize the flat pairs as well, and prepare them in a way that minimizes the skew of the distribution to create a pseudo-flat distribution out of this commonly taken run type.  
We also seek to verify and compare the results of the two calibrations, which we will explicitly show in Section \ref{sec:results}.

To scale the flat pairs due to the uneven density of exposures, we utilize a rolling mean scale method. 
Since the earliest region of the ADC is most highly peaked and then the total signal falls off by orders of magnitude, it is necessary to have a method which preserves the inherent structure but also reduces the overall amount of signal per bin.
For each window of 100 bins in the histogram we calculate the mean of the window, with starting bin i
\begin{equation}
\label{eq: rollingmean}
x_{i} = \sum_{i}^{i+100}(c_{i})
\end{equation}
where $c_{i}$ is the original occupancy of bin i. 
This is repeated for the entire range of the ADC. 
Next we calculate the rescale value of each histogram bin $c_{i}^{*}$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq: rescaled}
c_{i}^{*} = (3000*c_{i})/x_{i}
\end{equation}
The value 3000 is selected as the average value of the distribution beyond 50k ADU. 
We also prevent amplification of the signal if the prefactor is greater than 1, and preserve the original content of the bin.
The result of this scaling can be shown as the right plot in Figure \ref{fig:13144Distributions}. 
The results demonstrate an effective dampening of the peaked early region of 13144. 
Combining the rolling scaling with the trimming, we have two distributions representing the behavior of the ADC, which can be used to calibrate the ADCs as seen in the next section. 
As an alternative method to scaling, we try a dynamic prescale method described in Section \ref{sec:Dynamic Prescaling} in the appendix. 

\section{Cumulative Binning Calibration}
\label{sec: Cummulative binning}
\indent 


In this calibration, we seek to calibrate the ADCs without fabricating false INL and DNL by utilizing the flat pairs and ramp type EO testing runs. 
In this calibration, we will start by preparing the data as detailed in Section \ref{sec: Preparation}. 
We will then discuss the formal definition of the histogram distribution to include consideration of the probability distribution function (PDF). 
We will then consider our filter and define its relation to the PDF in order to equate the histogram signal to our model ADC. 
Finally, we will then use these definitions to construct the bin edges along the full range of the ADC. 
Throughout this process we will point out several considerations to be mindful of if trying to recreate such a calibration, such as the removal of particular bins, protection of the distribution from the filter, and other aspects crucial to calibration, as well as discussion of where to find a version of code used in this work.


\subsection{PDF definition and determination of ADC edges} 	
\label{sec:PDFEdges}
\indent 


In this section we develop a calibration process using the understanding of $\varrho$, the probability distribution function (PDF) in relation to our signal in ADU as mentioned in Section \ref{sec: Preparation}. 
Each bin of the ADC will have counts which correspond to the integral of the PDF, from the left edge of the bin to the right edge of the bin described as
 \begin{equation}
c_o =  \int_{l}^{r} \varrho dx
\label{eq:pdfcounts}
\end{equation}
where l is the left edge of the ADC, r is the right edge. 
There is also a relationship between the filtered distribution values and the PDF,
 \begin{equation}
f_i =  \int_{x_i}^{x_{i+1}} \varrho dx
\label{eq:pdffilter}
\end{equation}
where $f_{i}$ is a filtered output per $ADU_{i}$. 
The filtered value is equivalent to the integral of the pdf from the integer left to right edges of the pdf over a region of width 1. 
In this calibration we use a Savitzky-Golay filter with a window size of 33 and order 3. 
Using this description of the filter, we can utilize the fundamental theorem of calculus to examine how an integral of the pdf behaves over larger regions of the ADC. 
 \begin{equation}
F_{i}=\sum_{i=0}^{j} f_i= \int_{0}^{x_{j+1}} \varrho dx
\label{eq:fidef}
\end{equation}
Since our ultimate goal is to determine the edges of the ADC, l and r in equation \ref{eq:pdfcounts}, we need to interpolate non-integer values of these integral. 
This is done by fitting a cubic spline, $ \zeta$, to the cumulative sum, $ F_{i}$, which allows us to evaluate the integral of the PDF with non-integer end points, representing the true edges of the ADCs. 
Mathematically, we decompose the integral of the observed distribution to be:
 \begin{equation}
c_o = \zeta(r) - \zeta(l)
\label{eq:countsspline}
\end{equation}
where $\zeta(l)$, $\zeta(r)$ is the value of the spline at the left, right edge respectively.
Rearranging eq. \ref{eq:countsspline}: 
 \begin{equation}
\zeta(r) = c_o + \zeta(l) 
\label{eq:splineright}
\end{equation}
To determine the first right edge, we declare the first left edge is the earliest populated bin as described in Section \ref{sec:Recommended start}. 
We take $c_{o}$ to be the signal from the histogram in the first bin as described in Section \ref{sec: RDF}. 
We then compute the exact value of the spline for the desired occupancy, via Equation \ref{eq:splineright}.
We compare this exact value to that of the spline if the binwidth was exactly 1. 
If the value is too small/large we increase/decrease the bin size by a standard amount and recheck the value. 
This process is repeated until a convergence interval is reached, $\pm$1 count.
See the appendix for further information about the convergence criteria in Section \ref{sec:Convergence Interval}. 
The method then moves to the next left edge, the one just determined by the method, and successively places all the bin edges.
Together this yields a calibration of a single amplifier's ADC in under 10 seconds, without making any calculation or assumptions about the DNL and INL.

\subsection{Verification of no skipped bins} 
\label{sec: AD}
\indent 


Through developing this calibration, some issues arose which required attention in order for the calibration to not fail. 
We pay special attention to the specification from the manufacturer that the ADCs have no skipped bins\cite{ADCSpec}, by checking for missing codes and managing sparsely populated codes. 
In this section, we examine how this declaration is processed and examined in the method. 

Our first consideration involves the occupancy of each bin included in the distribution. 
We caution that low occupancy bins may represent Poisson noise rather than true signal from the ADC. 
To mitigate this risk, we remove any bin with less than 100 counts, which are bins with signals that are 1/30 of a typical bin.
This protects the calibration from exceptionally small bins due to noise, and recommends binning to begin where the distribution has ample signal to model. 

The second consideration concerns looking for skipped bins, either inherently in the distribution or as a result of the low occupancy exclusion as discussed in the prior paragraph. 
From the specifications sheet, the ADCs used in this calibration are specified to have no skipped bins throughout their range \cite{ADCSpec}. 
This implies all ADU values should be represented from in a combined distribution. 
We want to verify no skipped bins are present in our ADCs, and want to flag the amplifier if they are found. 
To do this, we run a verification over all the bins in the source histogram, looking for skipped codes and report any amplifiers in the calibration process of the ADCs.
In our method, if a skipped bin is found, one of two things will occur. 
If the skipped bin is an early ADU value, the bin is excluded from the calibration and the recommended start will be after that skipped bin.
For later skipped bins, we record their locations, and flag the amplifier to determine explicitly if the bin is truly missing or the result of a removed code. 
Together, this not only preserves the behavior of the ADC, but prevents the accidental termination of the method or interpolation over a bin with insufficient data for calibration. 

\subsection{Bounds to the calibration in ADU}
\label{sec:Recommended start}
\indent 


In this section we aim to recommend where to start and end the calibration utilizing the cumulative binning method.
We make recommendations and explain the choice for the first left edge used in Section \ref{sec:PDFEdges}, as well as a recommendation for where to terminate calibration.
Our recommendation is made considering doing two simultaneous calibrations in order to compare the results of the calibrating runs as discussed in Section \ref{sec:results}. 
This could be adjusted if the desired outcome is to calibrate as much of the ADC as data is available for. 

We will first discuss what ADU to recommend as the first left edge. 
We note our requirements for the first starting bin as follows: the minimum occupancy of such ADU must be above a specified threshold of 100 counts, there must be no skipped bins beyond the location of suggested start, there should not have a peak included in this distribution, and the ADU must be in both distributions. 
The first and second criteria are detailed in Section \ref{sec: AD}, and the fourth is motivated by the desire to compare the calibration of the two runs against each other. 
In order to compare the two calibration, the calibration must occur over the same range of ADU codes, otherwise the comparison will not be 1:1. 
The third requirement, of no peaks, arises from the behavior of the Savitizky-Golay filter and the degree to which it does a poor job characterizing the distribution when there is a large slope bin to bin of the source distribution. 
To mitigate this, we recommend examining the histograms for peaks using a peak finding algorithm, we used scipy \cite{SciPy}.
We then exclude the peak region, by recommending the start over 50 ADU of any peak detected within the source histogram. 
This may be adjusted depending on the specific dataset used, but in our data this was sufficient. 
This is less than 1 percent of the data and prevents synthetic INL and DNL injection from poor filter behavior, while still allowing calibration for the majority of the ADC. 
This protects the filter from largely misrepresenting the distribution, and preserves as much of the low signal distribution as possible.


We also make a recommendation for where to end the calibration. 
Generically, we take a recommendation of 90k ADU as the ending for most amplifiers, but this is adjusted down if a peak is detected at the end of either distribution, as discussed in the previous paragraph. 
In our data, we find the later ADU peaks are wider on average than any observed at earlier ADU.
To account for this, we recommend ending the calibration before hitting one such peak in order to prevent issues from the filter, and recommend about 100 ADU before such a peak is observed.
One could preserve more of the ADC in the low and higher region, by using a smaller filter window, such as 17 or 15, and we leave this knowledge here should someone desire a lengthier calibration.
We caution using a smaller window must only be done after verifying no high bit DNL exists, otherwise the filter will hide any affect. 
We discuss the filter choice more in the appendix \ref{sec:Filter}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\indent


Using the ADC calibration process described in Section \ref{sec: Cummulative binning}, we are able to calibrate all 3024 non-corner ADCs on the LSST Camera focal plane using both runs 13144 and 13549. 
The runs are compared by selecting the first left starting edge for both calibrations to be a bin with suitable information to start the binning process, as described in Sections \ref{sec: AD} and \ref{sec:Recommended start}.  
This requires both calibrations to begin at the same ADU value, which allows us to compare the results of each run. 
We will demonstrate the robustness of this method across runs by examining individual substructure, width and edge location differences, edge location differences as a function of starting bin, differential nonlinearity, and integral nonlinearity.


\subsection{Substructure}
\label{sec:substructure}
\indent 

For each individual amplifier’s ADC we can plot the binwidth as a function of ADU as seen in Figure \ref{fig:substructureplot}. 
The plot demonstrates that the spread in binwidths is between (0.65, 1.35) for both calibrations for our sample amplifier. 
This plot also demonstrates macroscopic groupings of binwidths through this region of the ADC. 
These groupings are repeated throughout the range of the ADC, seen in the bottom row plots of Figure \ref{fig:substructureplot}, showing that the structure of the ADC is periodic.

Our result also demonstrates that for this amplifier, R22S00C01, there are small bins every 128 codes. 
These small bins, or downticks, correspond to the same ADU values where the histogram demonstrated low occupancy, as seen in Figures \ref{fig:13144Distributions} and \ref{fig:13549Hist}.
This actively demonstrates that the method recovers the characteristics of the source distribution. 
This downtick behavior is found in many of the other amplifiers not shown here as well.
Since the method recovers this fingerprint, this indicates that the real behavior of the ADC is also recovered through the method developed in Section \ref{sec: Cummulative binning}. 
We find this also suggests an early clue both calibrations do a sufficient job representing the data as both have this feature present. 

Another notable aspect of Figure \ref{fig:substructureplot}, are the slight differences in the binwidth distribution by calibrating run. 
As seen here, there are some differences between the left and right plots, or the exact locations of the edges, stemming from minute differences of the calibrating run.  
This is most clearly seen in the clumping of binwidths for widths greater than 1, while there is good agreement for binwidths less than 1. 
We will explore in Section \ref{sec:Left Differences} the specific edge location differences of the in more detail, but note it here as a structural difference.  

We also note that the spread of the binwidths themselves are matched between calibrations. 
This is further shown in the bottom row of Figure \ref{fig:leftwidthADU}, where we examine bin by bin the difference in the binwidths calculated from the two calibrating runs. 
The histogram of these differences shown demonstrates a mean width difference by run of 0, with a maximum spread of $\pm$ 0.2. 
This suggests there is some dependence on the width determination as a function of the source histogram, but on average the methods are not deviating by a systematic amount.

\subsection{Edge Placement Differences}
\label{sec:Left Differences}
\indent 

To understand the exact deviations of the edge placements, we examine the locations of the left edges and binwidths seen in Figure \ref{fig:substructureplot}. 
We will accomplish this by first examining any deviations of the left bin edges from the two runs for a single amplifier. 
We will then explain any differences  in the binwidths by ADU for this same single amplifier. 
We will then examine any dependence which may exist between the starting bin ADU and the left/width differences.  

We begin by desiring to examine the location of one of the edges for each bin of the ADC as calibrated by either of the runs. 
By convention, we select using the location of the left edge. 
This choice is motivated by the method, in that we place the first left edge for both calibrations at the same ADU.  
To examine this, we first start by taking the difference in the location of the left edge of each bin. 
This is determined by running the calibration on both runs starting from earliest selected bin as described in Section \ref{sec:Recommended start}, then subtracting one set of left edges from the other, specifically the ramp determined edges from flat pair determined edges. 
The result of such a method is shown in Figure \ref{fig:leftwidthADU}. 
The distribution of left edge differences is shown in the top row. 
The plot demonstrates the left differences themselves, bin to bin, form a normal distribution centered around its mean -0.07, the leftmost plot. 
We see that the left edges differ between (-0.3, 0.2).  
This means on average that the bins from the ramp run calibration, 13549, are slightly larger on average than that of the bins from the rescaled flat pairs 13144 for this amplifier when binning starts on the earliest possible bin.
We attribute this difference due to the forced estimation of the first left edge to be somewhere in the distribution. 
That is, the first placed ADU bin edge may not exactly be where we place it, but without a prior information about the location of the bin edges, the method must begin somewhere. 
We will further examine this nonzero deviation of the mean later in this work. 
We also note the lack of structure when examining difference as opposed to its corresponding ADU, both on a large scale (middle plot) and early region (right plot).
This suggests that the two edges clearly follow each other throughout the calibrations.

Doing a similar analysis, but instead subtracting the widths of each distribution by ADU code, we can examine how well the two runs calibrate the binwidths themselves. 
We create a width difference distribution by taking the width of each ADU bin and then subtracting one width from the other. 
This is plotted as the bottom row of Figure \ref{fig:leftwidthADU}.
The width differences themselves have a mean of -1.8e-6, and are also normally distributed as seen in the leftmost plot.
They feature a range of binwidth difference between(-0.2, 0.2) over the range of the ADC. 
When plotted by ADU, the width difference makes no discernible pattern in near or long-term visualizations, seen in the middle and rightmost plots. 
This suggests the method is robust in calibrating the binwidths, and the binwidths closely follow from one calibration to the other. 

To further check for differences, we look to see whether a pattern exists of the calibration differences over the ADC. 
In Figure \ref{fig:Leftstarting} we plot the left edge difference as a function of ADU as a function of starting ADU value for 1000 successive ADU values. 
Not included is the width difference dependence, due to its negligible mean difference on the order of 1e-6 as shown in Figure \ref{fig:leftwidthADU}.
The plot instead features the left edge difference as a function of starting ADU bin. 
The distribution demonstrates there is some variation of the left edge difference as a function of the starting bin.
The mean left differences sits between (-0.15, 0.15) over the range of 1000 successive bin starts, with a mean of these means of 0. 
This verifies that there is some dependence on the calibration based on where the first left edge is placed. 
Furthermore, there is some dependence on this first bin, but the deviation on average is less than $|0.2|$ regardless of starting bin, suggesting the method does a sufficient job for both calibrations to align.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:Future}
\indent

The work presented in this paper outlines the ability to calibrate each of the LSST Camera's ADC on the focal plane.
The results of such calibration demonstrate both DNL and INL within the specifications set by the manufacturer. \cite{ADCSpec}
We note the ADCs appear to be stable, as the comparable results of the edge placements, binwidths, DNL, and INL values per bin between the two calibrations, 13144 and 13549. 
This stability is also through time, as  the exposures in the runs were taken in December 2021 and November of 2023 (13144 and 13549 respectively).

More generally, this method serves as a method to calibrate any ADCs. 
As projected in Figure \ref{fig:idealADC}, this method allows the conversion of bin edges from integer to real values, allowing for greater precision in measurements.
We include a truncated version of a look up calibration table for our sample amplifier as an example in Table \ref{tab:Table1}.
We note that we subtracted a pedestal of 26818 to align these values with zero for ease of comprehension.
The table provides the specific two edges corresponding to the given ADU, or the x axis of Figure \ref{fig:idealADC}. 
A recreation of the ideal plot can be seen as Figure \ref{fig:1314413549Edges}, where the edges of ADC are shown in blue from the flat pairs (13144) and orange from the ramp (13549). 
We see clearly here the differences discussed in Section \ref{sec:results}, and see the gain in understanding from conducting this calibration. 
We also see the ease to which one could use such a table to recover the true ADC edges.

As shown in this work, we have seen differences between the calibrations provided by the calibrating run for the ADCs. 
Based on our results, we recommend any calibration of ADCs be completed using a ramp equivalent method. 
We recommend this due to the need to heavily rescale flat pair data in order to find a suitable ADC calibration, although both show good agreement in edge placement. 

We reiterate here that this work demonstrates a calibration method for any general ADCs, with LSST Camera as the focal point. 
Our method demonstrates great consistency between two calibrating dataset types in terms of binwidths, DNL, INL, and we provide a sample calibration look-up table for clarity.

\section{Appendix}
\label{sec:appendix}
\indent 


In this section we detail additional details to some effects we mentioned earlier in this work. 
Specifically here we discuss an earlier attempt at a DNL ADC calibration, another form of prescaling the flat pairs via a dynamic prescale, discuss the convergence interval for placing the right edge, and then finally discuss the filter choice used in the method. 
We include these notes to aid any future ADC calibrations as well as justify many of our choices in our method. 

\subsection{First Pass DNL ADC Calibration}
\label{sec:Adrianmethod}
\indent 


This section details a DNL informed ADC calibration which was developed by A. Shestakov, which is a starting point for the work described in Section \ref{sec: Cummulative binning}. 
Using run 13144, as shown on the left of Figure \ref{fig:13144Distributions}, first a Savitizy-Golay filter with a window of 65 and order 3 was used to model an ideal ADCs behavior. 
The DNL of each bin was then computed, using the a tentative definition of the DNL  
 \begin{equation}
 DNL = \frac{c_e}{c_o}
 \label{eq:dnlfrac}
 \end{equation}
where the $c_{e}$ is the expected number of counts from the filter, and $c_{o}$ is the observed number of counts from the true distribution. 
Equating equation \ref{eq: DNL} with equation \ref{eq:dnlfrac} we yield an equation for the widths along the ADC as
 \begin{equation}
 w = 1-DNL = 1- \frac{c_e}{c_o}
 \end{equation}
Assuming the first left edge correspond to the first ADU with signal, the edges of the bins were placed accordingly as
 \begin{equation}
r = l + w 
\end{equation}
where r is the right edge and l is the left edge. 
This allowed the calibration of the ADC over a range of approximately 26-100k ADU, which is roughly bias through saturation of the CCD.  

This method yields a calibrated ADC where both INL and DNL have deviations from specification. 
Here we will break down the nonlinearities independently to parse where they come from in this calibration. 
The left side of Figure \ref{fig:originalDNLINL} demonstrates the DNL as computed using this method from earliest possible bin. 
One can clearly see that for low ADU there are many large, greater than $|0.5|$ ADU DNLs, yet later on the DNL falls within a more expected range. 
This translated into early bin widths being very narrow while later bins are much wider. 
We break the reasoning for this phenomena into two parts. 
The first is the indication that the filter itself struggles to characterize a true ADC for low ADU.
That is the $c_{e}$ are small on average early on as compared to the data point they are fitting.
This can be explicitly shown by plotting the w65o3 filter onto the histogram itself. 
This issue disappears when the filter starts beyond the peak near bias, suggesting there is a distribution based issue for the filter fitting the data accurately, an issue we will examine closely in Section \ref{sec:Filter}.

There is also structure in the INL resulting from this method described in Section \ref{sec:Adrianmethod}.
The right plot in Figure \ref{fig:originalDNLINL} demonstrates unbounded INL over the range of the ADC, with a maximum deviation of over 140 ADU. 
Clever cuts to the distributions successfully lowered INL contributions to 10 ADU, but reduced the range of the calibration significantly to cover a range of about 30,000 ADU. 
This suggested that no amount of clever cuts would be able to coax both the INL and DNL into specifications. 

Another notable dependence arose between the starting bin and the maximum magnitude INL contribution achieved over the calibration. 
It is noted, but not shown, that a shift of 1 ADU in starting location could result in maximum INL change of over 100 over the range of the calibration. 
The would suggest that the starting location in ADU was highly dependent on the calibration returned by this early method, an instability which is detrimental for robust calibrations. 
Together these issues with INL and DNL indicated the need for a more robust treatment of the calibration method, which we develop in this work.

\subsection{Dynamic Prescaling Flat Pairs}
\label{sec:Dynamic Prescaling}
\indent 


Before developing the rolling scaling method  used in Section \ref{sec: Preparation}, we attempted to utilize a dynamic prescaling factor to combines portions of all the exposures from a flat pair run into a combined distribution which is relatively flat over the entire range of the CCD. 
In practice we examine the region of the ADC where the combined signal is relatively flat, typically higher ADU codes, and select the saturation level for the entire distribution. 
For run 13144 we used 5000 counts per ADU. 
We then compute a prescale factor, $\chi _i$, for each 25 ADU subregion of the ADC, where
\begin{equation}
\chi_i = \frac{5000}{c_{av}}
\label{eq: prescale}
\end{equation}
where $c_{av}$ is the average observed counts from the combined histogram over the subregion. 
For any regions of the ADC where the $c_{av} < 5000$ the pre-scale factor was taken to be 1, or all data in that region was used for the combined distribution. 

Each exposure is then pulled and its peak signal in ADU is matched with the corresponding pre-scale factor. 
Then the number of samples to select from that exposure, denoted as $\eta $, is determined via
 \begin{equation}
 \eta = \chi_i *n 
 \label{eq:selectedsamples}
\end{equation}
Where the number of pixels in the amplifier, n, is dependent on the type of CCD attached to that ADC, either an E2V or ITL from the two manufacturers of the CCDs which have slightly different numbers of pixels per chip. 
A randomization process selects $\eta$ pixel digitizations from the exposure, and these are added to the combined distribution. 
This is repeated for each exposure in the run, and builds a pseudo-flat distribution over the full range of the ADC while keeping the behavior in each exposure. 
This method worked sufficiently well, but was abandoned in favor of the rolling scaling used in Section \ref{sec: Preparation} for its ease and reduced computation time.

\subsection{Convergence Interval}
\label{sec:Convergence Interval}
\indent 


In this section we discuss the work done to evaluate the convergence interval used in the cumulative sum binning method. 
The method uses a convergence interval of $\pm$ specified value to converge on the right edge of the ADC bin, that is the degree to which equation \ref{eq:splineright} is exact.
To quantify this in a more exact way, we include this convergence interval, $\sigma$, into equation \ref{eq:splineright}. 
\begin{equation}
\zeta(r) \pm \sigma = c_o + \zeta(l)  
\label{eq:splineright}
\end{equation}

Through many iterations, we determined a decrease in $\sigma$ corresponded to an increase in computation time.  
For reasonable $\sigma$ values, we found no positive impact on the DNL or INL seen in the distribution. 
Thus, the convergence interval of 1 count optimizes the computation time while still yielding a 0.2 percent difference between the average observed counts and that achieved by the method. 
This difference is very precise with an uncertainty of any single bin average 1/3000, which is significantly lower than other errors which may occur.  

We also examined the result of the convergence integral, or the residual, to see whether this amount was significant in some way. 

either by the method going over or under the goal amount per bin, and whether some bias was present due to it. 
In this, we tested whether the residuals had some pattern to them over the range of the ADC.
It was determined that the residuals make no noticeable pattern over the range of the ADC, and rather also suggests that the bins were not larger or smaller than expected. 
Thus we ruled out that the uncertainty of 1 count was sufficiently low, while maximizing the computation time for all bins of a single ADC in under 30s. 

We also tested adding the residual to the next bin, as an ad hoc method of dealing with the residuals. 
The results of such calibration found no noticeable changes in the DNL.
Yet new structure emerged in the INL, suggesting adding the residuals caused misplacement of the next adjacent bin. 
This can be understood by thinking of adding the residual changing the next bin size, and iterating over 100k bins, resulting in an extreme case over the range of 100k counts, which will affect where the bin edge will be placed.
These changes, coupled with 0.2 percent difference suggests the residuals were of a negligible amount and no more robust treatment of them is necessary. 

\subsection{Filter Choices}
\label{sec:Filter}
\indent 

We also examined the use of many filters to determine Savitizy-Golay w33o3 was the best choice to use in this method. 
Our goal was to find a filter which represents the data clearly over the range of the CCD. 
Early work demonstrated the filter tends to undervalue in peaked regions, as alluded to in Section \ref{sec:Adrianmethod}. 
Thus we examine many filters, windows and order of fit to understand this relationship clearly. 
With the Savitzky-Golay filter we tried various window sizes between 25 and 100 as well as both 2nd and 3rd order variants. 
We also tried another type of filter, Butterworth with high-pass, lowpass, and bandpass. 
We utilized Chi-Squared tests in order to determine which filter best represented the input data and distributions, using both the real data used in this paper, and fabricated datasets as well. 
The results demonstrated that the Savitzky-Golay filter with window size 33 and order 3, represented all the data very well, and was therefore chosen for use in the method.  
We also tried smaller window sizes, such as 13, 17, and 19, as suggested in Section \ref{sec:Recommended start}, to examine using more of the early region of the ADC.
We found success also with these smaller windows, but do not implement them due to concern about higher bit DNL being masked by the smaller window.
