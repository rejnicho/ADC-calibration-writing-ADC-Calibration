\begin{abstract}
    This work outlines a calibration of Analog to Digital Converters (ADCs) using a differential nonlinearity informed method.
Specifically, this method was developed for use in Rubin Observatory's LSST Camera but could be applied to other ADCs. 
We name this method the cumulative binning method.
Critically, the method examines the long range behavior of the ADC and places the bin edges according to the probability distribution function (PDF). 
The calibrated results showcase the individual periodic, braided structure of each ADC by examining and modeling its long range behavior. 
The calibration recovers important quantities of the ADCs, specifically differential and integral nonlinearity which fall within specifications from the manufacturer, Analog Devices (\cite{AnalogDevices}). 
We also demonstrate our robust calibration utilizing two types of testing data from the electro-optical testing runs for the LSST Camera, a strength of this method to allow various data types to be used.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec: Motivations}

The Vera C. Rubin Observatory is a one of a kind observatory, uniquely designed as the descendant of many great predecessors such as the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) known for the volume of its survey to ¼ the sky (\cite{Almeida2023}), the Dark Energy Survey (DES) known mapping millions of galaxies (\cite{bechtol2025}). 
Located in Cerro Pachón, Chile, Rubin Observatory’s Simonyi Survey Telescope is a modified Paul-Baker design with a 8.4m primary mirror. 
The design allows a 9.6 square degree field of view, which will observe in 6 broadband optical filters, ugrizy. 
Rubin Observatory will begin the Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) in 2025 to capture over 20,000 square degrees, with nearly 1000 visits to each location in the Southern sky over the 10 year survey. 
The survey strategy includes 30s visit times to achieve such coverage, and produce the wide, fast, deep, survey. 
The project aims to examine 4 central science themes: Probing Dark Energy and Dark Matter, Taking an Inventory of the Solar System, Exploring the Transient Optical Sky, and Mapping the Milky Way (\cite{Ivezic2019}).

Rubin Observatory will feature the LSST Camera, the largest digital camera ever made. 
The camera has 3200 megapixels over 189 scientific, fully depleted charge coupled devices (CCDs) and 12 corner CCDs acting as wavefront guides (\cite{Arndt2010, Gilmore2008}). 
The CCDs used in LSST Camera have two manufacturers, E2V and ITL. 
CCDs are a well studied device (\cite{Janesick2001}), used in precursor camera systems such as Hubble's Wide Field Camera (\cite{Wyckoff1993}), Dark Energy Survey's DECam (\cite{Flaugher2015}), and Subaru's Hyper-Suprime Camera (\cite{Miyazaki2018}).
The LSST Camera's CCDs are 100\textmu m to balance infrared sensitivity while minimizing diffusion in the sensors (\cite{OConnor2006}).

The CCDs are arranged into rafts as 3x3 arrays, with groups of 3 CCDs connected to a readout electronic board (REB) (\cite{OConnor2016, Lopez2018}).
Each pixel has a physical size of 10\textmu m x 10\textmu m and a plate scale of 0.04 arcsec sq/pixel (\cite{Doherty2014}). 
Rubin Observatory’s survey plan aims for a full readout of the focal plane in under 2s to minimize dead time, so each 1.6 megapixel CCD is segmented into 16 amplifiers which are read out in parallel (\cite{Lange2024}), requiring separate electronics.

The sensors were characterized through extensive testing electro-optical testing completed at SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory to understand quantities such as quantum efficiency, bright and dark defects, and charge transfer inefficiencies (CTE)  before integration (\cite{Kotov2016, Doherty2014}).
The camera was then assembled at SLAC, and the entire focal plane was studied in electro optical (EO) testing to measure the bias, saturation, and linearity, among other properties (\cite{Roodman2024}). 
The voltages and timing electronics for the entire system are also optimized for best operations and clarity of images to be taken on sky (\cite{Utsumi2024}). 
Additional studies have investigated camera anomalies and interesting aspects such as weather (\cite{Banovetz2024}), the brighter fatter effect (\cite{Broughton2024}), and crosstalk (\cite{Snyder2024}). 

The understanding of the entire camera system is integrated into an instrument signature removal (ISR) pipeline to recover accurate signal incident to the sensors (\cite{PlazasMalagon2025}). 
From Plazas Malagon et al. (\cite{PlazasMalagon2025}), we clearly see that a gap exists in the ISR pipeline for the Differential Nonlinearity (DNL) correction, but a look-up table could sufficiently fill this missing area. 
Previous work such as for the Hubble Wide Field Camera demonstrates consideration for the ADC correction but does not clearly outline such a method for its computation and correction (\cite{Wyckoff1993}).
This work aims to fill that gap by creating an ADC calibration utilizing a DNL informed method. 

We will develop a calibration for each ADC used by Rubin Observatory's LSST Camera, which are Analog Devices 18-bit, 1 MSPS PulSAR ADCs (\cite{AnalogDevices}).
In this calibration, we will emphasize the importance of the differential nonlinearity (DNL) and the integral nonlinearity (INL), in order to properly represent the ADC behavior and match the specifications set by the manufacturer. 

In the following sections we will develop our method as outlined here. 
Initially in Section \ref{sec: Motivations}, we will define DNL and INL, specify the criteria for calibrating data, and describe the data preparation process. 
We will explain the cumulative binning method via the probability distribution function in Section \ref{sec: Cummulative binning}.
Then we will examine the result of our calibration in Section \ref{sec:results}, noting interesting differences between calibration data. 
Section \ref{sec:Future} will describe how to apply our calibration, and includes a sample look-up table for the ADC correction. 
Finally, the appendix will note additional considerations for this calibration as well as an earlier trial calibration of the ADCs.

\subsection{Definition of Differential and Integral Nonlinearity}
\label{sec: DNL_INL_Def}

The analog to digital converter, as the name implies, converts the analog signal from a system into a digital one. 
The output signal is measured in analog to digital units (ADU). 
The input signal has limits, called edges, which map the input voltage range to a stated ADU value. 
These edges have a lower bound, called the left edge, and an upper bound, called the right edge.
The difference between the right edge and the left edge determines the width of the bin, and is determined through the process called calibration of the ADC. 
This calibration is dependent on the intrinsic illumination of the sensor which the ADC is attached, which, through the calibrated gain, specifies the conversion between this illumination and the ADU.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{ADCModel.png}
    \caption{A schematic demonstrating how the analog to digital converter ideally works (red), and its true fabricated behavior (black). On the y axis is the ADU value recorded by the ADC, which has integer values. On the x axis is the edge of the ADC, or the left and right edges of the bin which maps to a given ADU. As tracked along the red, an ideal ADC has bins with integer edges which map to corresponding ADU. The black shows a simulated true ADC, which has left and right edges that deviate from the ideal, but still maps to a single ADU.}
    \label{fig:idealADC}
\end{figure}

As seen in Figure \ref{fig:idealADC}, in a perfect ADC (red), the bins have a width of exactly 1 ADU, and the edges are integers. 
That is, an input signal 0-0.9 would be 0 ADU, 1.0-1.9 is 1 ADU, and so on. 
However, in a true ADC (black), bins may differ from the ideal bin width of 1 ADU and therefore may have non-integer left and right edges mapping to specific ADU values. 
As shown in Figure \ref{fig:idealADC}, our fabricated ADC voltage 0-1.2 would be 0 ADU, 1.3-1.8 is 1 ADU, and so on. 

The presence of deviations from an ideal ADC in terms of bin widths leads to two types of nonlinearity: differential nonlinearity (DNL) and integral nonlinearity (INL). 
DNL is the deviation of a bin's width from that of an ideal bin width and is defined exactly as
\begin{equation}
DNL =1- w  
\label{eq: DNL}
\end{equation}
where w is the width of a bin, and the ideal binwidth is set to 1. 
This value can be computed for all bins along the ADC. 
DNL values are typically on the order of $\pm$ 0.5, and any value greater than $\pm$ 1 could indicate a skipped bin or ADU codes. 
The manufacturer of the ADCs used on the LSST Camera, Analog Devices, state they have no missing codes from the manufacturing (\cite{AnalogDevices}), which will be verified in this analysis. 

The second type of nonlinearity is integral nonlinearity. 
This is the deviation of the middle of a bin from that of an ideal bin, which is denoted as
\begin{equation}
INL = m_{ideal} - m_{adc} 
\label{eq: INL}
\end{equation}
where $m_{ideal}$ is the midpoint of an ideal bin, and $m_{adc}$ is the midpoint of an ADC bin. 
The midpoint of an ideal bin is ADU+0.5. 
INL typically yields contributions from each bin on the order of $\pm$ 1, with deviations above 2 being out of specification for our study(\cite{AnalogDevices}). 

If we compute the DNL and INL for our fabricated data shown in Figure \ref{fig:idealADC}. 
For the first bin, the DNL = 1.2-1 = 0.2, and INL = 0.5-0.6 = -0.1. 
These values fall within the normal expected range.

We note the importance of these nonlinearities, as they characterize the ADC.
Patterns in these values could reveal structure in the ADC, as the DNL and INL can be used to examine the degree to which an ADC calibration is accurately representing the ADC. 
For instance, a local portion of the ADC with DNL may also induce some local INL, by definition.
While we may expect the behaviors to be correlated, changes in one type of nonlinearity but not the other may signal an issue in the calibration or in the ADC itself.
Therefore, we will examine any structure that is present through our calibration of LSST Camera's ADCs, especially any nonlinearity outside of the expectation. 

\subsection{Run Selection and Filters}
\label{sec: RDF}

Calibration of the ADCs for LSST Camera involves using a flux range of signal from the CCD, from bias to saturation, which is mapped to roughly 100k ADU. 
The CCDs collect charge as a function of incident light on the sensor, meaning any one image could only show some small subset of the flux range of the CCD and therefore only an equally small subset of ADU.
To cover all the codes of the ADC, a large number of exposures are needed to represent the flux range of the CCD. 
For our use case of the LSST Camera, we utilize collections of test exposures called runs as collected through EO testing at SLAC. 
Runs completed during the EO testing period are designed with specific calibrations and characterizations as their focus, which help inform ISR corrections(\cite{PlazasMalagon2025}). 
Therefore, in this calibration, we seek to use data suitable for understanding the ADC behavior.

The first data run we use is 13144, which features over 600 exposures of flat pairs with illumination covering bias to saturation of the CCDs. 
When compiled into a single histogram in counts per ADU, we can look at the distribution of codes represented by the ADC.  
Figure \ref{fig:13144Distributions} demonstrates one such combined distribution for our example amplifier, R22S00C01.
This plot shows variations in the amount of signal present in each bin over the target range of the ADC.
The bin-to-bin deviations are two-fold. 
The most present is the right skew of the signal, which corresponds to a high density of exposures with low exposure time.
This structure is the result of the log-based flux sampling used for the flat pairs and is coupled with the Poisson distribution spreading the distribution about its mean.
This results in a two order of magnitude difference in the signal near bias as compared to saturation.
This difference will have to be scaled out in order to use this run for our ADC calibration, as discussed further in Section \ref{sec: Preparation}. 
On a smaller scale, we note the presence of local bin-to-bin deviations that denote the presence of DNL in the ADC, as seen in the upper portion of Figure \ref{fig:13144Distributions}. 

The second type of run for this calibration is a ramp run, 13549. 
This run was designed specifically to calibrate the ADCs by looking at a general ramp up of signal. 
This was accomplished by allowing the sensors to continue to integrate charge while the focal plane was read out. 
Resulting in exposures with a base illumination plus a ramp upwards. 
This is repeated three times for each of the 78 exposure times, spaced 2.2s apart, to cover the flux range of the signal into ADC from the CCDs.
Our sample distribution for our reference amplifier is shown in Figure \ref{fig:13549Hist}, demonstrating the the CCD's even coverage over this range.

Next, we must consider how to model an ideal ADC.
This will allow us to compute DNL and INL, and compare our calibration to expectations.
We want this model to represent the long range behavior of the ADC, such as our observed 128 bin low bins structure seen in Figure \ref{fig:13549Hist}, while minimizing bin-to bin deviations.
We note in the appendix Section \ref{sec: BitFractions} the fraction of bits represented is mostly even over the range of the CCD, with deviations only seen in bits 0 and 1.
This allows us to safely claim and look for DNL is in the two least significant bits (LSB). 
Therefore, we use a Savitizy-Golay filter  with a window size of 33 and polynomial order 3 as this model. 
We justify the use of a filter outside the order of the DNL we are interested, while still following our data. 
More details on selecting the filter type is discussed in the appendix Section \ref{sec:Filter}. 

We overlay the filtered values (red) over the combined histograms for the flat pairs and ramp runs as seen in the right plot of Figure \ref{fig:13144Distributions} and Figure \ref{fig:13549Hist}.
Effectively, this demonstrates the preservation of long range behavior and smoothing on smaller scales. 
We will later use this filter as the model ADC to develop the method outlined in Section \ref{sec: Cummulative binning}. 

\subsection{Run Preparation}
\label{sec: Preparation}


With the suitable data types demonstrating signal over the range of bias to saturation of the CCDs, we must prepare that data to be calibrated. 
We require an even density of exposures to infer occupation in ADC bins; that is, the density of information must be consistent in order to prevent an arbitrarily skew the bin size. 

To more completely understand the density motivation we consider how signal is expected to fall into each of the ADC bins. 
We call the distribution of ADU values for a given amount of light the probability distribution function (pdf).
This is how many pixels on the amplifier will yield a specific ADU value given some accumulated charge. 
We have no \textit{a priori} knowledge of this distribution and instead need to infer it from the signal we receive from the ADC.
As determined using the calibration method in Section \ref{sec: Cummulative binning}, the shape of the calibrating distribution contributes to the characterizing parameters such as the DNL and INL.
Therefore, utilizing a distribution where there is a large amount of exposures in a single flux region and few in another creates small bins (on average) with large DNL and an unstable (bin starting dependent) INL as seen in Section \ref{sec:Adrianmethod} in the Appendix. 

Given this need for a flat distribution and the base structure of our calibrating runs, we must prepare our runs for use. 
The first step in our preparations involves "trimming" the exposures to remove the signal from the pixels that fall on the outside edges of the CCDs. 
These pixels have diverging electric fields which cause the signal in this physical region to be less than its true signal. 
The need to conduct this trimming originates from a small peak in signal near the bias level in corner amplifiers, which could not be attributed to how the data was collected. 
To preserve as much signal as possible while removing this defect, we trim 2 pixels from each edge of the CCD in every exposure.
Testing demonstrated that 2 pixels was sufficient in removing this defect, while trimmings between 0-12 pixels were examined.
For consistency, and because a potential bias level peak would be masked in the skewed distribution of the flat pairs, we trim all exposures in both the ramp and flat pairs runs. 
This also allows for the preservation of as much signal as possible for the ADC calibration. 
We note that removal of the picture frame has also been studied as part of the ISR for LSST science image preparation, which differs from the requirement to conduct this calibration. 

Next we must address the variable density of representation in 13144, or the left plot of Figure \ref{fig:13144Distributions}. 
This problem was the motivating factor for the development of 13549, which was taken after this work to calibrate the ADCs began. 
While the ramp run avoids the density problem, it is not always practical to request a special run of exposures for this calibration given the time necessary for the run.
Therefore, we move to be able to utilize the flat pairs as well, and prepare them in a way that minimizes the skew of the distribution to create a pseudo-flat distribution out of this commonly taken run type.  
We also seek to verify and compare the results of the two calibrations, which we will explicitly show in Section \ref{sec:results}.

Since the earliest, lowest-flux region of the ADC is most highly peaked, it is necessary to have a method which preserves the inherent structure but also reduces the overall signal per bin in a way that is proportional to the original signal.
To scale the flat pairs due to the uneven density of exposures, we utilize a rolling mean scale method. 
For each window of 100 bins in the histogram we calculate the mean of the window, with starting bin i
\begin{equation}
\label{eq: rollingmean}
x_{i} = \sum_{i}^{i+100}(c_{i})
\end{equation}
where $c_{i}$ is the original occupancy of bin i. 
This is repeated for the entire region of the ADC we seek to calibrate. 
Next we calculate the rescale value of each histogram bin $c_{i}^{*}$
\begin{equation}
\label{eq: rescaled}
c_{i}^{*} = (3000*c_{i})/x_{i}
\end{equation}
The value 3000 is selected as the average value of the distribution beyond 50k ADU. 
We also prevent amplification of the signal if the prefactor is greater than 1, and preserve the original content of the bin.
We justify the use of the window of size 100 in Section \ref{sec: BitFractions}, where we examine bit behaviors outside the region of the rescaling and find no notable features. 
The result of this scaling can be shown as the right plot in Figure \ref{fig:13144Distributions}. 
The results demonstrate an effective dampening of the low-flux region of 13144. 
We note an alternative method to scaling, a dynamic prescale method described in Section \ref{sec:Dynamic Prescaling} in the appendix, also could be used to rescale. 

Combining the rolling scaling with the trimming, we have two distributions representing the behavior of the ADC, which can be used to calibrate the ADCs as seen in the next section.  

\section{Cumulative Binning Calibration}
\label{sec: Cummulative binning}



In this section we will formally define the cumulative binning method for calibration of the ADCs. 
We will discuss the formal definition of the histogram distributions, as seen in Figures \ref{fig:13144Distributions} and \ref{fig:13549Hist}, and relate these to the probability distribution function (PDF). 
We will then consider our filter and define its relation to the PDF in order to equate the histogram signal to our model ADC. 
Finally, we will use the mathematical descriptions of the histogram and filter to recover the bin edges of the ADC. 
We also provide several considerations which are critical to a successful calibration, such as the removal of particular bins, protection of the distribution from the filter, and other aspects crucial to calibration.
In the final subsection we share a version of code used in this work.

\subsection{PDF definition and determination of ADC edges} 	
\label{sec:PDFEdges}


In this section we develop a calibration process using the understanding of $\varrho$, the probability distribution function (PDF) in relation to our prepared signal in ADU as mentioned in Section \ref{sec: Preparation}. 
Each histogram bin corresponding to an ADU has a specified count which is the integration of signal in the bin from its left to right edge described as
 \begin{equation}
c_o =  \int_{l}^{r} \varrho dx
\label{eq:pdfcounts}
\end{equation}
where l is the left edge of the ADC, r is the right edge. 
There is also a relationship between the filtered distribution values and the PDF,
 \begin{equation}
f_i =  \int_{x_i}^{x_{i+1}} \varrho dx
\label{eq:pdffilter}
\end{equation}
where $f_{i}$ is a filtered output per $ADU_{i}$. 
The filtered value is equivalent to the integral of the PDF from the integer left to the integer right edges of the PDF, a width 1. 
In this calibration we use a Savitzky-Golay filter with a window size of 33 and order 3. 
Using this description of the filter, we can utilize the fundamental theorem of calculus to examine how an integral of the PDF behaves over larger regions of the ADC. 
 \begin{equation}
F_{i}=\sum_{i=0}^{j} f_i= \int_{0}^{x_{j+1}} \varrho dx
\label{eq:fidef}
\end{equation}
Since our ultimate goal is to determine the edges of the ADC, l and r in equation \ref{eq:pdfcounts}, we need to interpolate non-integer values of these integrals. 
This is achieved by fitting a cubic spline, $ \zeta$, to the cumulative sum, $ F_{i}$. 
The cubic spline allows non-integer integration bounds, representing the true edges of the ADCs. 
Mathematically, we decompose the integral of the observed distribution to be:
 \begin{equation}
c_o = \zeta(r) - \zeta(l)
\label{eq:countsspline}
\end{equation}
where $\zeta(l)$, $\zeta(r)$ is the value of the spline at the left, right edge respectively.
Rearranging eq. \ref{eq:countsspline}: 
 \begin{equation}
\zeta(r) = c_o + \zeta(l) 
\label{eq:splineright}
\end{equation}
To determine the first right edge, we declare the first left edge is the earliest populated bin as described in Section \ref{sec:Recommended start}. 
We take $c_{o}$ to be the signal from the histogram in the first bin as described in Section \ref{sec: RDF}. 
We then compute the exact value of the spline for the desired occupancy, via Equation \ref{eq:splineright}.
We compare this exact value to that of the spline if the binwidth was exactly 1. 
If the value is too small/large we increase/decrease the bin size by a standard amount and recheck the value. 
This process is repeated until a convergence interval is reached, we use $\pm$1 count for the results shown in Section \ref{sec:results}.
We examine in the Section \ref{sec:Convergence Interval} more of the details of the convergence interval.
The method then moves to the bin, using the right edge just computed as the new left edge. 
This is repeated for the range of the ADC desired. 
Together this yields a calibration of a single amplifier's ADC in under 10 seconds, without making any calculation or assumptions about the DNL and INL.

\subsection{Bounds to the calibration in ADU}
\label{sec:Recommended start}


In this section we aim to recommend where to start and end the calibration utilizing the cumulative binning method.
Our recommendation is made considering doing two simultaneous calibrations in order to compare the results of the calibrating runs as discussed in Section \ref{sec:results}. 
This could be adjusted if the desired outcome is to calibrate as much of the ADC as data is available for. 

We will first discuss what ADU to recommend as the first left edge. 
We note our requirements for the first starting bin are as follows: the minimum occupancy of such ADU must be above 100 counts, there must be no skipped bins beyond the location of suggested start, peaks should not be present in the histogram distribution of counts, and the ADU must be in both distributions. 
The first and second criteria are detailed in Section \ref{sec: AD}.
The fourth is necessary to compare the calibration of the two runs against each other as both calibrations must occur over the same region of ADU codes to be 1:1. 
The third requirement, is a bit more sensitive.
It is the result of the behavior of the Savitizky-Golay filter.
We note the SG filter underestimates the bin occupancy when there is a large bin to bin slope of the source distribution. 
To mitigate this, we recommend examining the histograms for peaks using a peak finding algorithm and excluding ADU within 50 ADU of a peak detected in the histogram.  
This may be adjusted depending on the specific dataset used, but in our data this was sufficient.
We note that this limits our calibration, in our specific case removing less than 1 percent of the data. 
Yet, the benefit of avoiding this peak and the improper placement of the lowest ADC edges prevents synthetic INL and DNL injection. 
This protects the method from largely misrepresenting the ADC, and preserves as much of the low signal distribution as possible.
After all 4 requirements are met, any first bin edge is sufficient for this calibration.  
Specifically, in this work and in the examples shown for the amplifier R22S00C01, we choose to begin our calibration a few bins beyond the absolute minimum recommended. 
This choice to begin the binning at 26825 is motivated by the desire to minimize the left edge deviations between the two runs which is shown and explained in great detail in Figure \ref{fig:Leftstarting}.


We also make a recommendation for where to end the calibration. 
Generically, we take a recommendation of 90k ADU as the ending for most amplifiers, but this is adjusted down if a peak is detected at the end of either distribution, as discussed in the previous paragraph. 
In our data, we find the later ADU peaks are wider on average than any observed at earlier ADU.
To account for this, we recommend ending the calibration before hitting one such peak in order to prevent issues from the filter, and recommend about 100 ADU before such a peak is observed.
One could preserve more of the ADC in the low and higher region, by using a smaller filter window, such as 17 or 15, provided DNL is not observed at those specific bit levels. 
We leave this knowledge here should one desire such a calibration.
We caution using a smaller window must only be done after verifying no high bit DNL exists, otherwise the filter will hide any affect. 
We verify no higher bit DNL should be observed in the appendix Section \ref{sec: BitFractions}.

To recover more of the ADC in a generic system, one could inject charge to the front end electronics to get the ADC response for more ADC codes.
This method was examined for the LSST Camera by C. Jurmay, but requires disassembly of the camera to isolate the ADCs. 
For an already integrated system, we recommend a multistage approach.
First one should use a large filter over a smaller range of the ADC to check for higher bit DNL, and verify this against some digitization check such as bit fractions. 
Then redo the edge placement with a smaller filter window, still beyond focus DNL, to probe into earlier ADU. 
Continue this approach until all ADU desired are covered. 
In principle one could also use the baseline starting and ending ADU and then use the same format to calculate the preceding left edges or the proceeding right edges beyond these bounds. 

\subsection{Verification of no skipped bins} 
\label{sec: AD}


In this section we focus on the specification from the manufacturer that the ADCs have no skipped bins (\cite{AnalogDevices}), by examining our data and calibration for missing codes and managing sparsely populated codes. 
Herein we describe how to check for such a situations and how to treat this situation. 

We will first discuss issues with sparsely populated codes.
The ADCs are subject to Poisson noise due to their function digitizing signal of counts of photons.  
This noise could manifest as bins with very low occupancy, and caution that low occupancy bins may represent Poisson noise rather than true signal from the ADC. 
To mitigate this risk, we remove any bin with less than 100 counts, which are bins with signals that are 1/30 of a typical bin.
This protects the calibration from exceptionally small bins due to noise, and recommends binning to begin where the distribution has ample signal to model. 

The second consideration concerns looking for skipped bins, either inherently in the distribution or as a result of the low occupancy exclusion as discussed in the prior paragraph. 
From the specifications sheet, the ADCs used in this calibration are specified to have no skipped bins (\cite{AnalogDevices}). 
This implies all ADU values should be represented in a combined distribution, if this is not the case we want know what amplifier's ADC is having this issue. 
To do this, we run a verification over all the bins in the source histogram, looking for skipped codes and report any amplifiers in the calibration process of the ADCs.
In our method, if a skipped bin is found, one of two things will occur. 
If the skipped bin is an early ADU value, the bin is excluded from the calibration and the recommended start will be after that skipped bin.
For later skipped bins, we record their locations, and flag the amplifier to determine explicitly if the bin is truly missing or the result of a removed code. 
Together, this not only preserves the behavior of the ADC, but prevents the accidental termination of the method or interpolation over a bin with insufficient data for calibration.

We reiterate here that this work demonstrates a calibration method for any general ADCs, with LSST Camera as the focal point and its results shown explicitly.

\subsection{First Pass DNL ADC Calibration}
\label{sec:Adrianmethod}

This section details a DNL informed ADC calibration which was developed by A. Shestakov, which is a starting point for the work described in Section \ref{sec: Cummulative binning}. 
Using run 13144, as shown on the left of Figure \ref{fig:13144Distributions}, first a Savitizy-Golay filter with a window of 65 and order 3 was used to model an ideal ADCs behavior. 
The DNL of each bin was then computed, using the a tentative definition of the DNL  
 \begin{equation}
 DNL = \frac{c_e}{c_o}
 \label{eq:dnlfrac}
 \end{equation}
where the $c_{e}$ is the expected number of counts from the filter, and $c_{o}$ is the observed number of counts from the true distribution. 
Equating equation \ref{eq: DNL} with equation \ref{eq:dnlfrac} we yield an equation for the widths along the ADC as
 \begin{equation}
 w = 1-DNL = 1- \frac{c_e}{c_o}
 \end{equation}
Assuming the first left edge correspond to the first ADU with signal, the edges of the bins were placed accordingly as
 \begin{equation}
r = l + w 
\end{equation}
where r is the right edge and l is the left edge. 
This allowed the calibration of the ADC over a range of approximately 26-100k ADU, which is roughly bias through saturation of the CCD.  

This method yields a calibrated ADC where both INL and DNL have deviations from specification. 
Here we will break down the nonlinearities independently to parse where they come from in this calibration. 
The left side of Figure \ref{fig:originalDNLINL} demonstrates the DNL as computed using this method from earliest possible bin. 
One can clearly see that for low ADU there are many large, greater than $|0.5|$ ADU DNLs, yet later on the DNL falls within a more expected values. 
This translated into early bin widths being very narrow while later bins are much wider. 
We break the reasoning for this phenomena into two parts. 
The first is the indication that the filter itself struggles to characterize a true ADC for low ADU.
That is the $c_{e}$ are small on average early on as compared to the data point they are fitting.
This can be explicitly shown by plotting the w65o3 filter onto the histogram itself. 
This issue disappears when the filter starts beyond the peak near bias, suggesting there is a distribution based issue for the filter fitting the data accurately, an issue we will examine closely in Section \ref{sec:Filter}.

There is also structure in the INL resulting from this method described in Section \ref{sec:Adrianmethod}.
The right plot in Figure \ref{fig:originalDNLINL} demonstrates unbounded INL over the range of the ADC used, with a maximum deviation of over 140 ADU. 
Clever cuts to the distributions successfully lowered INL contributions to 10 ADU, but reduced the scope of the calibration significantly to cover a range of about 30,000 ADU. 
This suggested that no amount of clever cuts would be able to coax both the INL and DNL into specifications. 

Another notable dependence arose between the starting bin and the maximum magnitude INL contribution achieved over the calibration. 
It is noted, but not shown, that a shift of 1 ADU in starting location could result in maximum INL change of over 100 over the range of the calibration ADU codes. 
The would suggest that the starting location in ADU was highly dependent on the calibration returned by this early method, an instability which is detrimental for robust calibrations. 
Together these issues with INL and DNL indicated the need for a more robust treatment of the calibration method, which we develop in this work.

\subsection{Dynamic Prescaling Flat Pairs}
\label{sec:Dynamic Prescaling}


Before developing the rolling scaling method  used in Section \ref{sec: Preparation}, we attempted to utilize a dynamic prescaling factor to combines portions of all the exposures from a flat pair run into a combined distribution which is relatively flat over the entire flux range of the CCD. 
In practice we examine the region of the ADC where the combined signal is relatively flat, typically higher ADU codes, and select the saturation level for the entire distribution. 
For run 13144 we used 5000 counts per ADU. 
We then compute a prescale factor, $\chi _i$, for each 25 ADU subregion of the ADC, where
\begin{equation}
\chi_i = \frac{5000}{c_{av}}
\label{eq: prescale}
\end{equation}
where $c_{av}$ is the average observed counts from the combined histogram over the subregion. 
For any regions of the ADC where the $c_{av} < 5000$ the pre-scale factor was taken to be 1, or all data in that flux region was used for the combined distribution. 

Each exposure is then pulled and its peak signal in ADU is matched with the corresponding pre-scale factor. 
Then the number of samples to select from that exposure, denoted as $\eta $, is determined via
 \begin{equation}
 \eta = \chi_i *n 
 \label{eq:selectedsamples}
\end{equation}
Where the number of pixels in the amplifier, n, is dependent on the type of CCD attached to that ADC, either an E2V or ITL from the two manufacturers of the CCDs which have slightly different numbers of pixels per chip. 
A randomization process selects $\eta$ pixel digitizations from the exposure, and these are added to the combined distribution. 
This is repeated for each exposure in the run, and builds a pseudo-flat distribution over the full flux range of the CCD. 
This method worked sufficiently well, but was abandoned in favor of the rolling scaling used in Section \ref{sec: Preparation} for its ease and reduced computation time.

\subsection{Convergence Interval}
\label{sec:Convergence Interval}


In this section we discuss the work done to evaluate the convergence interval used in the cumulative sum binning method. 
The method uses a convergence interval of $\pm$ specified value to converge on the right edge of the ADC bin, that is the degree to which equation \ref{eq:splineright} is exact.
To quantify this in a more exact way, we include this convergence interval, $\sigma$, into equation \ref{eq:splineright}. 
\begin{equation}
\zeta(r) \pm \sigma = c_o + \zeta(l)  
\label{eq:splineright}
\end{equation}

Through many iterations, we determined a decrease in $\sigma$ corresponded to an increase in computation time.  
For reasonable $\sigma$ values, we found no positive impact on the DNL or INL seen in the distribution. 
Thus, the convergence interval of 1 count optimizes the computation time while still yielding a 0.2 percent difference between the average observed counts and that achieved by the method. 
This difference is very precise with an uncertainty of any single bin average 1/3000, which is significantly lower than other errors which may occur.  

We also examined the result of the convergence integral, or the residual, to see whether this amount was significant in some way. 
In this, we tested whether the residuals had some pattern to them over the range of used ADU codes in the ADC.
It was determined that the residuals make no noticeable pattern over the range of  ADU, and rather also suggests that the bins were not larger or smaller than expected. 
Thus we ruled out that the uncertainty of 1 count was sufficiently low, while maximizing the computation time for all bins of a single ADC in under 30s. 

We also tested adding the residual to the next bin, as an ad hoc method of dealing with the residuals. 
The results of such calibration found no noticeable changes in the DNL.
Yet new structure emerged in the INL, suggesting adding the residuals caused misplacement of the next adjacent bin. 
This can be understood by thinking of adding the residual changing the next bin size, and iterating over 100k bins, resulting in an extreme case with over 100k counts, which will affect where the bin edge will be placed.
These changes, coupled with 0.2 percent difference suggests the residuals were of a negligible amount and no more robust treatment of them is necessary. 

\subsection{Bit Fractions}
\label{sec: BitFractions}

In this section, we motivate the use of the rescaling the flat pairs run, 13144, on order of 100 and use of the Savitsky-Golay filter's window size of 33 in our method. 
We will examine both these claims by examinging a quantity we term "bit fraction". 
The bit fraction is a representation of how often a specific bit, as the binary representation of an ADU code, is turned on. 
In an unbiased ADC, we would expect that the bit fraction is exactly 0.5 for all bits. This corresponds to an even amount of times where the bit is turned on and turned off. 
We calculate the bit fraction as the following: 
\begin{equation}
b_x = n_x/N 
\label{eq:bitfract}
\end{equation}
where $b_x$ is the bit fraction for the xth bit, $n_x$ is the number of times this bit is turned on, and N is the total number of codes examined. 
We could examine this fraction for all 18 bits of our ADCs, but choose to limit our examination for this paper to bits 0-7 to examine the range of 0-128. 
This range is suitable for our rescaling region and our filter window. 

We first examine our need to rescale the flat pairs, 13144, in window sizes of 100.
To do this, we compute the bit fractions for the unscaled 13144 and compare to the ramp run 13549. 
The bit fractions are computed for bits bits 0-7 as in Figure \ref{fig:Tworunbitfrac}. 
Examining that figure, we see that for the ramp run, 13549, bits 0 and 1 are the least well behaved, with no notable excursions beyond these two bits. 
We note that the behavior is different for the unscaled flat pairs, 13144. 
We note some excursions seen in bits 6 and 7, which are not present in the ramp run.
We interpret this result to being inherent to the data taking, rather than the ADC as if a characteristic of the ADC it should be seen in both runs.
We further use this to justify the use of rescaling in the flat pairs to see the true behavior of the ADC rather than a print through of the data taking method.
Since there is no notable bit fraction deviations beyond the 2 least significant bits (LSB), we can scale our data well outside this window and use 100 as this window size in the calibration. 

In Figure \ref{fig:Singleampbitfrac} we seek to examine if a regional dependence of the bit fractions exists for the ramp run 13549. 
We select doing this analysis on the ramp run to avoid issues with data shape impacting our bit fractions, as noted earlier the data shape impacts which ADC codes are more/less represented. 
The plot demonstrates for all regions of the CCD, the 2 Least Signficant Bits (LSB) are the least well behaved, and we note no regional dependence exists in the bit fractions. 
This illustrates the well behaved nature of the ADC beyond the 2 LSB. 
We note that for the 0s exposure we do not plot the bit fractions for bits 7 due to poor sampling. 
This allows us to select the window size of 33 to be well beyond the local DNL we wish to measure and recover in the method, and use it in our filter to model an ideal ADC.

We note, but do not explicitly show, that this result of the 2 LSB being the least well behaved is robust over all examined amplifiers shown in this work.

\subsection{Filter Choices}
\label{sec:Filter} 

We examined the use of many filters to determine Savitizy-Golay w33o3 was the best choice to use in this method. 
Our goal was to find a filter which represents the data clearly over the flux range of the CCD. 
Early work demonstrated the filter tends to undervalue peaked regions, as alluded to in Section \ref{sec:Adrianmethod}. 
Thus we examine many filters, windows and order of fit to understand this relationship clearly. 
We compared various filters to our data as well as fabricated data using a chi square goodness of fit. 
We used the following types of filters in our tests; Savitzky-Golay: windows 25-100, 2nd and 3rd order polynomials, Butterworth: highpass, lowpass, and bandpass. 
The results demonstrated that the Savitzky-Golay filter with window size 33 and order 3, represented all the data examined very well, and was therefore chosen for use in the method.  
As an additional comment, a smaller window size  such as 13, 17, and 19, would allow one to more carefully probe lower order DNL and potentially recover a calibration closer to the bias leve.  
We found success with these smaller windows, but do not implement them due to an abundance of caution regarding masking out higher order DNL by the smaller window.
